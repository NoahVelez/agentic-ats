Proposal in Class 9. Final presentations in Classes 13–14. This is the main event: an end-to-end agentic AI system your team scopes, builds, hosts, and defends.

What to build (requirements)

A multi-agent system. One agent is not enough — the project must coordinate more than one.
At least one enrichment, chosen from: RAG, MCP, a tool/API integration, human-in-the-loop, or an LLM-as-judge.
A working UI (Streamlit is recommended — it is the shortest path from Python to something people can click).
Hosted publicly as a portfolio-ready artifact, with a live URL anyone can open.
All of it in your team’s GitHub repository, with the secrets discipline from the repo section.
An incident log. A running record of what broke, kept in the repo alongside the code. Two kinds of entry: discovered (it broke on you) and induced (you broke it deliberately, to find an edge). At least four induced entries by the final presentation. Each entry records what you did, what you expected, what actually happened, what the system showed you at the time, what you changed, and how you know the change worked.
The arc

Proposal (Class 9): a short pitch — the problem, why it genuinely needs an agent, the intended tools and approach, and where it will live in the repo. Graded (10%).
Build: your team builds across the back half of the semester.
Final presentation (Classes 13–14): the working project, presented and defended live. Assessed on both the project and the quality of the presentation.
Working as a team

Dividing and coordinating the work is part of the grade. This is the workforce lesson: you will not always pick your teammates, and making a shared deliverable come together is a real skill. See the group rules below.

How Groups Form
Groups of 3, assigned randomly by the instructor. You do not pick your teammates — this mirrors real teams you are placed on at work.
You get your group in Class 5 (Sep 28). That is when the random assignment happens and the clock on the final project starts.
If class size is not divisible by 3, the instructor will form one or two groups of a different size and note it at assignment.
If a teammate drops or is persistently absent, tell the instructor early; groups may be rebalanced. Coordination problems are expected — raising them early is part of handling them.

Grading Rubrics
These describe what strong work looks like for each graded component. They are guides to what is valued, not point checklists — judgment is applied to the whole.

Rubric
| Criterion | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| **Meets requirements** | Multi-agent, at least one enrichment, a working UI, hosted publicly with a live URL. |
| **It works** | The system runs and does what it claims. Where it fails, the team knows why and says so. A demo that hides known failure modes scores lower than one that names them. |
| **Technical quality** | Sound design; the multi-agent split and enrichment are used for real reasons, not decoration. |
| **Presentation & defense** | Clear presentation; the team explains and defends design choices under questions. |
| **Teamwork** | Evidence the work was shared and coordinated (commit history, division of labor). |
| **Incident log** | Shows real diagnosis, not "we fixed it." The induced failures are genuine attempts to find edges rather than staged ones. The team can say what they still cannot prove. |
